Retrieval · a measurement problem

Where the Cut Falls

Before a search system can find an answer, somebody has to decide where to cut the document up. That decision is usually made by accident, and it sets a ceiling on everything downstream. Here is what it costs, and how to measure it.

The problem, in one paragraph

Suppose you want a program to answer questions about your company's documents. It cannot read all of them for every question — there is far too much text — so it stores them in pieces and fetches only the pieces that look relevant. Those pieces are called chunks, and cutting the documents into them is called chunking.

Nothing about a document tells you where the cuts go. Most systems default to something arbitrary: every 800 characters, say. And that works fine, right up until a cut lands in the middle of the sentence that holds the answer.

question — “When does the Northeast file?” highlighted = the answer │ = a cut, every 90 characters
The Northeast filing deadline is the one exception. Every other region reports quarterly, on the fifteenth day of the month following the close of the quarter. The Northeast files annually instead, on 31 March, because its state-level requirements were never harmonised.

That third cut is the whole problem. The answer is now split across two chunks. Fetch either one and you get half a fact — “files annually instead, on 31” — which is worse than useless, because it looks like an answer.

Why you cannot just make the chunks smaller

The obvious fix is tighter chunks, so less irrelevant text comes back with each answer. But shrink them too far and the answer gets severed more often, not less — and a chunk with no context around it can become unreadable on its own. A chunk starting “It files annually instead” has lost the word Northeast entirely.

So it is a trade, and the trade moves depending on your documents and on the questions people ask. Which means it is something to measure, not something to have an opinion about.

The measurement

Here is the trick that makes this tractable. Don't run the whole system and judge its answers — that confounds the chunker with the search engine and the language model, and you learn nothing about any of them. Instead, mark the exact characters that answer the question, and ask a much narrower question about the cuts alone:

Given where this chunker put its boundaries, what is the best any search engine could possibly do?

That number has a name — Precision Ω, “precision omega”. Take every chunk that contains any part of the answer, imagine you retrieved all of them perfectly, and ask what fraction of the text you'd be holding is actually the answer. It is a ceiling. No retriever, however good, can beat it, because the boundaries were fixed before it ever ran.

Drag the sliders. The answer is 49 characters long; everything else you retrieve alongside it is padding. Notice what overlap does — it is supposed to stop answers being severed, and it does, but every extra copy of the text is more padding you have to carry.

Why recall lies

The intuitive measure is recall: did we retrieve the answer at all? It is also nearly useless on its own, and the cleanest demonstration is an accident.

One of the strategies we tested splits documents at their headings. Run it on a transcript of a speech — which has no headings — and it produces exactly one chunk: the whole document. That chunk contains the answer to every question. Its recall is a perfect 100%.

Perfect recall, and completely useless: to answer “when does the Northeast file?” it hands over the entire speech. A metric that treats a chunk as a single yes-or-no unit scores that a flawless hit. You have to measure inside the chunk to see the problem at all.

The part where we got it wrong

Precision Ω comes from a technical report by Chroma. Our implementation was written from a description of it rather than from the source, because the session doing the research couldn't reach the original. The description was ambiguous in one specific way, and we guessed.

We assumed it meant the smallest set of chunks that covers the answer. It actually means every chunk containing any part of the answer. Those are the same thing — right up until chunks overlap, at which point the second is larger and the score is lower.

The report publishes its own numbers, and Precision Ω needs no search engine to compute, so we could simply check. Both readings, on the same 472 questions:

Look at the last column. With no overlap it is almost exactly right. With overlap it is about 28% too high — and overlap is precisely the setting people reach for when answers keep getting severed. The wrong version would have passed every test we thought to write, looked entirely reasonable, and quietly recommended turning overlap up.

A published table is a better test than any number of tests written against your own understanding of what the number means. The one check that could catch this, and it cost a 1.6 MB download.

What the evidence actually says

Three things, none of which is what the marketing around this topic suggests.

The boring default is hard to beat

Splitting on paragraph and sentence boundaries at around 200 tokens, with no overlap, is consistently near the top on every measure. It never wins outright and it never embarrasses itself. Most of the elaborate alternatives are competing for the remainder.

Overlap is not free

Duplicating text between neighbouring chunks does reduce severed answers. It also inflates your index and drags the ceiling down hard — 13.9 against 17.7 for otherwise identical settings. It is a real trade, not a safety setting to leave on.

“Semantic” chunking depends entirely on your documents

Using a language model to find topic boundaries is the expensive, sophisticated-sounding option. A 2024 study found it gives no consistent benefit — but the useful finding is buried past the headline. On documents that jump between unrelated topics it wins enormously (81.9 against 69.5 on one benchmark). On ordinary documents, where related sentences sit near each other anyway, it loses.

Which is the entire argument for measuring rather than choosing: the ranking depends on your corpus, so it has to be computed on your corpus.

Things you can check before any of this

Some faults need no questions, no marked answers and no model at all — just the document and the proposed cuts. They are cheap enough to run on every candidate configuration before deciding which are worth evaluating properly:

  • Does any text vanish? A chunker that drops a trailing paragraph produces a run that succeeds and scores that look fine.
  • Do cuts land inside sentences? On one document, arbitrary fixed-size cutting landed on a natural boundary 6% of the time. Splitting at headings: 83%.
  • Are tables and code blocks being cut in half? A table severed from its header row is unusable, and nothing about the average chunk size will ever tell you.
  • Do chunks start with “as described above”? A chunk opening with a reference to something outside itself has been cut off from what it needs.
The limit worth stating. These can tell you a configuration is broken. They cannot tell you which of two reasonable configurations is better — “good chunking” is only defined relative to the questions people actually ask. Anything claiming a universal chunk-quality score without queries is overselling.

The tool

chunking-lab is the instrument all of the above came out of. It takes documents, runs them through a dozen chunking strategies, and scores each one. The whole default path is deterministic and offline: no API key, no model, no network.

# screen strategies with no questions and no model
chunking-lab metrics report.md \
    --strategy fixed:800/400 --strategy recursive:200 --strategy structural

# rank them against marked answers
chunking-lab score --strategy recursive:200 --strategy structural

# check the headline metric against a published table
make chunking-benchmark

None of the mechanism here is new — screening chunkers on cheap structural signals is published work, and so is correlating those signals against retrieval quality. What this one does differently is need no model to do it, and reproduce someone else's published numbers exactly, so you can tell whether to believe it.
